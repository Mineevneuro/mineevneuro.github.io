Patient-specific 3D-printed surgical guides

10 clinical cases at Almazov National Medical Research Centre: drill guides, cutting guides, a vertebroplasty guide and anatomical models designed from the patient's CT/MRI and used in the operating room.

Role
Built the pipeline; segmentation, design, printing, intraoperative use
Setup
Picaso Designer X Pro (FDM) in the neurosurgical department, PLA, low-temperature plasma sterilisation (Sterrad)
Software
Materialise Mimics (most used), 3D Slicer, Inobitec, ITK-SNAP; Blender for design
Period
2021–2025; ~10 paediatric cranial cases in residency, 2020–2021

Clinical context

Spinal navigation was not routinely available in the department, so patient-specific guides were the default way to bring a CT-based plan into the operating room. Three recurring problems:

  • Pedicle and translaminar screws in altered anatomy (metastatic vertebrae, cervicothoracic junction, C2 lamina), where freehand angulation error risks breach or cut-out.
  • En-bloc resection of sacral chordoma, where recurrence is linked to margin status and the osteotomy has to follow a planned margin.
  • Titanium cranioplasty, where contouring a plate to the defect during surgery is slow and imprecise.

Approach

  1. CT / MRI
  2. Segmentation (3D Slicer, Inobitec, Mimics, ITK-SNAP)
  3. Trajectory & guide design (Blender)
  4. STL
  5. FDM print, PLA
  6. Sterrad sterilisation
  7. Operating room
  • Drill guides: screw trajectories modelled as cylinders through the vertebra; the guide's contact surface is taken from the segmented vertebra at each level.
  • Cutting guides: sacrum and tumour segmented; the resection plane is set along the planned margin and turned into a slotted matrix for an ultrasonic bone scalpel. Version 2 (Case 3) changed the matrix geometry for better fit and stability.
  • Vertebroplasty guide: two channels set the needle trajectories into the vertebral body for open vertebroplasty.
  • Anatomical models: bone with vessels for approach planning (L4), skull with defect for pre-bending titanium mesh.

About 1.5 hours of hands-on work per case, plus about 10 hours of unattended printing.

Case 6, plan to result

Metastases at Th3 and Th5. Spine segmented from preoperative CT, screw trajectories set as cylinders in Blender, one drill guide per level fitted to the posterior elements. Decompression, then pedicle screws through the guides.

Case 6: screw accuracy

On postoperative CT the screw tips sit on average 2.3 mm from the planned trajectories, and no screw moved more than 1 mm toward the spinal canal. One of the four, Th3 right, is tilted about 12° in the craniocaudal direction. Details: guided screw accuracy on postoperative CT.

Earlier work: paediatric cranial cases in residency, 2020–2021

About 10 paediatric cases in residency: skull models for craniosynostosis surgery (cranial vault distraction, fronto-orbital reconstruction) and cranioplasty, printed on a Picaso Designer X Pro (FDM). Segmentation was first done by a member of the department staff, later by me, in 3D Slicer, Inobitec, ITK-SNAP and Mimics; Mimics turned out the most convenient. The operation was rehearsed on the model: osteotomies marked and cut, fragments fixed, distractors and implants fitted.

Verification and the regulatory view

A printed guide is a patient-specific medical device made inside the hospital. We had no formal quality system for it, but several of the checks a manufacturer would require came up in practice.

What we actually did

  • Sterilisation vs geometry. We tested it empirically: standard heat sterilisation softened the parts enough to deform them and shift the drilling trajectory. Low-temperature plasma sterilisation (Sterrad) kept the shape.
  • Material choice. PLA (polylactic acid) was chosen because the literature describes it as biocompatible; it is also one of the materials that tolerates low-temperature sterilisation.
  • Pre-use verification. Every guide was printed together with a bone model of the same patient. Before surgery the guide was inspected on its own (print defects, warping) and then seated on the model to check fit and trajectory.
  • Design iteration from use. The first sacral cutting guide was awkward to handle in the wound; the second version changed the geometry (Cases 2 → 3).
  • Clinical study. The cervical and thoracic drill guides were used within a hospital clinical study of 3D-printed guides for transpedicular fixation; the study documents were prepared by a senior resident.
  • Records. Blender projects and STL files were kept per case (G-code for some), so a guide can be traced back to its design.

What was missing

  • No microbiological testing (cultures) after sterilisation.
  • No systematic measurement of guide accuracy at the time. Measured later for one case (Case 6).
  • No written risk analysis or design-verification protocol: the checks above were done, but not documented as a procedure.

How I would set it up today

Under EU MDR, a guide made for one named patient is a custom-made device (Article 52(8), Annex XIII); in the US, the FDA's work on point-of-care 3D printing treats a hospital that prints devices as taking on manufacturer responsibilities. In practice that means:

  • a risk file per ISO 14971, with hazards such as guide mis-seating, deformation after sterilisation, segmentation error, wrong patient or wrong level;
  • design controls per ISO 13485: inputs (imaging protocol, target trajectory), outputs (STL, print settings), verification on the model, validation of the sterilisation process for each material;
  • if the planning step is automated, the planning software itself falls under IEC 62304.

Limitations / next steps

  • Case series from one centre, no control group; outcomes are per case, not a comparison with navigation or freehand.
  • Also tried, not developed further: patient-specific navigation guides for endoscopic transnasal chiasmal-sellar surgery (presented at the Nationwide Neurosurgical Forum, 2023). See below.
  • Screw accuracy against the plan is measured for one case only (Case 6, 4 screws).
  • Segmentation and guide design were manual: about 1.5 hours of hands-on work per case, plus about 10 hours of printing.
  • Next: automate the planning step, see automated pedicle screw planning.
